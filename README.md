**Reflection**

I mentioned some of the testing method above- but the majority of my testing would fall under functional testing. Specifically, it would be functional unit testing (JUnits!) as that’s really what this class is all about. I also applied some integration testing when it came to the 3 different applications working together. 
Really though I was very concerned with my setters and getters working well together. Another method of testing that I mentioned above was boundary value testing. This one was a total blind spot for me at first but luckily caught by one of my fellow students in our group. 
This specifically would be entering an incorrect date for an appointment- such as one that has already passed. Finally I channeled my inner evil developer and tried to just purposefully enter input that might break the system- like days that would be fall under leap years and thus not occur on a calendar this year or next. 
This type of test is often referred to as exception testing. 

When it came to testing that I didn’t try there were a lot actually! We only have so much time in the semester so it makes sense that we would overlook some. The first thing that came to my mind first was scalability testing. 
Our application could work if we were only getting a few hundred applications- but what if we were to get thousands of users. What if those users had the same exact data- how would the system handle something like that? Next, we did not test for performance at all. 
This one makes sense as we aren’t sure how the application will be run- this is also not something I am aware of could be tested by myself? I am fairly certain that there are ways to test for heavy traffic or users with low power machines- but that’s something I look forward to learning later. 
Finally, and most likely the most important- security. While I didn’t test for security, I was cognizant of it in the development process. I am the sole developer, and it would be easy for me to make a mistake here. This is something I think at a professional level would happen without question. 
Especially if we are handling personal information. In today’s internet ecosystem just a name and contact information could be enough to dox someone. So, this is something that will be a concern for me in the future.
Finally, how important is discipline in code structure? It is possibly the most important aspect of being a good developer. Discipline isn’t something that people can accidentally fall into- it is deliberate and practiced. 
An undisciplined developer can still write good code, but they will more often than not slow down the development process for others. When you write code that is buggy or with errors baked in you are relying on others solve you are not only hurting your team, but your own growth as a developer. 
Most professional developers will borrow code from github and other repositories, but doing so makes following your work very difficult and you could also be baking errors into your application that you might not even be aware of. 
Doing things the hard way isn’t fun and it takes time- but anything worth doing is hard. Keeping a series of notes as you work through a project I think is a good idea- specifically with your thoughts and intentions! 
Intentions being a key word here- telling other developers what you intended to write could help others coming onto a new project a lot. They might have a faster way of writing what your working on or they might be unfamiliar and it could be a learning experience for everyone. 
Really, I think that ultimately the best thing you can do as a developer is aim to write the cleanest code possible. Doing so will take longer- but should be shorter than a code review and debugging that happens afterwards. 
Demonstrating to people above you that your code is better (with proof) will give you a much greater chance of getting promotions or more experiences as a developer and minimizes your chances of potentially getting laid off- something unfortunately quite common in our current job market.
